Clima does not assume your organization has fixed levels. There is no «branch» field
and no «department» field: there is a **tree of nodes** that you build, with the
depth and the names your company actually has.

A retail chain might go `Country → Region → Store → Department`. A forty-person
consultancy might have two levels and stop there. Both are valid structures, and the
product treats them the same way.

## One single root, and the account is not part of the tree

Each organization has **exactly one root node**, and you create it yourself with
whatever name and type you want. It is not automatic and it is not the account: the
organization is who we bill, and it never appears in the structure.

The distinction looks like a detail and it is not. The account is a data boundary —
nothing from one organization is visible from another. The tree is an **analysis
tool**: you change it when the company changes, or when you want to look at the
results a different way.

## The node is the unit of everything else

Almost everything in the product hangs off a node:

- **Employees.** Every person in the roster lives in a node.
- **Questionnaires.** A questionnaire is assigned to a node and **its descendants
  inherit it**, so you do not have to assign it store by store.
- **The scope of a study.** A study measures a node and everything below it.
- **Permissions.** A manager is assigned a branch and sees that branch: not the rest
  of the tree, and not the results next door.
- **Results.** Every index is published per node. That is what lets you say «the
  Northern Region is eight points below average».

## The shape of the tree decides what you can compare

This is the part worth thinking about up front rather than afterwards.

Results are published **per node**. A cut that does not exist in the tree does not
exist in the results: if all the cashiers in a store hang off the same node as the
stockers, there is no way to look at them separately, however interesting the
question. And the other way round — a tree split into twenty nodes of six people
each produces twenty results, almost none of which can be published, because they do
not reach the confidentiality threshold.

> **It is a trade-off between granularity and publishability, and it has a hard
> floor.** Every node whose results you want to read has to gather enough responses
> to clear your organization's threshold. Before subdividing a branch, look at how
> many people are left on each side — see [Anonymity and minimum
> N](/en/docs/anonymity).

The demographic axes —sex, age band, tenure band— are the other half of the analysis
and **do not live in the tree**: they cross it. Which means you do not need a
«women under 30» node; that is a segment, and you build it while reading the results.

## Reorganizing without losing history

Companies reorganize, and the product accounts for it: nodes can be renamed,
reordered and moved. Before you delete one, the product tells you what goes with it
— how many employees, how many assignments and how many studies touch that branch.

What you **cannot** do is move the ground under a study that is currently measuring.
A study in progress has its scope and its instrument fixed from the moment it
launched, and that immobility is what makes its results mean anything — the same
reason you cannot edit a question halfway through.
