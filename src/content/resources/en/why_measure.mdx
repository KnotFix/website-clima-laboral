In many companies, a team's discontent is discovered the day someone resigns. By then
there is nothing left to ask: the decision is made, and almost nobody tells the whole
truth in an exit interview.

Measuring workplace climate and employee satisfaction exists precisely to find out
sooner. It is not an HR formality or a survey to tick a box: it is the cheapest way an
organization has to learn how its people are doing while something can still be done.

## What not knowing costs

The global numbers are well known, and they are not good. According to Gallup's
[State of the Global Workplace 2026](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx)
report, only **20%** of employees worldwide were engaged at work in 2025, the lowest
figure since 2020. Gallup estimates that in that year low engagement cost the world
economy **about $10 trillion in lost productivity**, equivalent to 9% of global GDP.

In a specific company, that cost has names:

- **Turnover.** Replacing one employee costs
  [between one-half and two times their annual salary](https://www.gallup.com/workplace/247391/fixable-problem-costs-businesses-trillion.aspx),
  and Gallup notes that this is a conservative estimate.
- **Resignations that could have been avoided.** In the same article, Gallup reports that
  **52%** of people who left voluntarily said their manager or organization could have done something to
  keep them.
- **What the spreadsheet does not show.** Absences, mistakes, friction between teams,
  customers poorly served. None of it is labeled "climate", but climate is almost always
  behind it.

The good part of those numbers is the one usually skimmed over: if more than half of
the people who leave believe something could have been done, there is a lot of room for
whoever finds out in time.

## Climate and satisfaction are not the same thing

They are used as synonyms and they are not. It is worth measuring both, separately.

- **Satisfaction** is how each person feels about their job: what they do, what they
  earn, how much they are recognized, whether they see a future there.
- **Climate** is how the environment they work in functions: leadership, communication,
  collaboration between teams, trust.

They can move in different directions. A team can be happy with its pay and still work
in an environment where nobody dares to say what they think. Another can get along
great with its manager and feel its work leads nowhere. If both are blended into a
single number, neither problem shows, and the action plan ends up aimed at the wrong
place.

## What an organization that measures gains

**It decides with data, not with hallway impressions.** What reaches management is
usually what the loudest people say, or what each team's manager believes is happening.
A well-run survey gives every person the same weight, including the one who would never
raise a hand in a meeting.

**It finds the problem where it is.** A company average can look fine and hide a team,
a shift or a site that is struggling. Looking at results by segment is what turns
"climate is so-so" into "the night shift at plant two needs attention".

**It knows whether what it did worked.** Without a measurement before and another after,
any initiative (a recognition program, a schedule change, leadership training) is judged
by gut feeling. Measuring regularly, and always the same way, turns those initiatives
into something you can verify.

**It gives people a voice, and that alone changes something.** Asking signals that each
person's opinion matters. On one condition: that something happens with what they said.

**And it shows in business results.** A study by Alex Edmans, published in the _Journal
of Financial Economics_ in 2011, followed the companies on the "100 Best Companies to
Work For in America" list from 1984 to 2009: their stocks returned **2.1 percentage
points a year more than those of their own industry**. The study's title says it all:
the market was slow to value employee satisfaction, because it does not appear on the
balance sheet.

## How to measure so the survey is useful

Measuring badly can be worse than not measuring: if people do not trust the survey, they
answer what is convenient, and the results give false comfort. These are the conditions
that matter most.

1. **Real anonymity.** Nobody answers candidly if they fear their manager will know what
   they said. That includes never showing results for a group so small that someone
   could guess who answered, even when filtering by position, sex or tenure.
2. **Reach everyone.** If the survey can only be answered from a computer, it leaves out
   people on the plant floor, in the field or in the warehouse, who often have the most
   to say. Email, mobile phones and a tablet at the workplace cover different cases.
3. **The same instrument every time.** A trend only counts if it was measured the same
   way at both points. Changing the questions from one year to the next leaves two
   snapshots that cannot be compared.
4. **A measurement with a date and a rhythm.** Once a year is the most common and the
   minimum to see a trend. Measuring more often than changes take to show wears people
   out.
5. **Share the results and act.** Share them soon, even if they are not good, pick a few
   topics, give each one an owner and a date, and tell people what changed before the
   next measurement. A survey with no visible action lowers participation in the next
   one.

## Where to start

It does not take a huge project. It takes deciding what you want to know, picking a
date, telling people why you are asking and committing to share what was done with the
answers.

What is worth avoiding is building it all by hand. Collecting responses, protecting
anonymity, looking by team and comparing against last year in loose spreadsheets turns
into weeks of work, and that is exactly where the mistakes that cost a survey its
credibility are made.
